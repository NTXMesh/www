Kite Nodes for Mesh Networking

Kite-lofted nodes are a cheap, battery-free way to dramatically extend mesh coverage and range. Height really is might — a relay floating a few hundred feet up can connect you to towns you'd never reach from the ground.

Kites are a great, low-cost alternative to drones. There are fewer restrictions to consider, but there are other challenges to keep in mind.

Considerations
Wind speed, kite type, line weight and length, node size and weight.
Pros
Cheap entry, long flight times (no batteries).
Cons
Wind- (nature-) dependent; possible to lose control (or wind) and suddenly crash.
Kite types
Delta, Parafoil, Box.

The Payload

Building the Kite Node

You want your kite relay node to be as lightweight as possible. I wanted to know the specific altitude, so I attached a RAK 12500 GPS — this is not required, though it came in handy later (see Lessons Learned). I placed the node, battery, and GPS into a resealable plastic bag and taped it to the kite. The total payload weight was 27 g.

Setup

Attaching the Node to the Kite

For my delta kite I saw two placement options: the underside (ground-facing) part that anchors to the line [left photo], or the topside (sky-facing) spreader pole [right photo]. I tried both and found the topside to be better. Using strong tape, I adhered one edge of the plastic bag, wrapped around the spreader pole, then back onto the other side of that same edge. Then I taped the opposite bag edge flat against the kite.

Setup

Reel & Line

I pre-marked my line at 100 ft intervals so I'd have a better idea of how much I let out — one ring mark at 100 ft, two marks at 200 ft, three at 300 ft, and so on. I also attached a short length of paracord with a D-clip to my reel, letting me anchor the reel to a chair once the kite was airborne so I could stay comfortable and hands-free to message the mesh. In the left photo below you can see a little black mark mid-way up the yellow line — that was 100 ft.

Flight Day

Getting Airborne

I went to a local park and was out there for a few hours, so I recommend bringing a chair and snacks. If you don't know how to get a kite airborne, YouTube is your friend (also trial and error). The delta-style kite is rather low-effort to launch and to keep in the air.

Results

Mesh Results

The kite node is several hundred feet above you — well out of Bluetooth range — so a ground node is a must. Connected to my ground node (as Client_Mute), I messaged the Default Meshtastic channel. That was carried to my 0-hop kite node (as Client / Router / Router_Late) and then relayed to the broader area mesh. I saw replies from nodes in the next town over on either side of me — Leander to Round Rock and Jonestown. My ground node's long name included "via Kite", so recipients knew there was a kite airborne. (This was before Router_Late and Unmonitored existed, so I'd probably use those today.)

If you can pre-plan a flight day and coordinate other kites or drones, you can really begin to test the capabilities of your local mesh coverage area. In my small town of Leander, just north of Austin, I'm in somewhat of a bowl with elevated hills surrounding me. This experiment showed me that a good, well-placed, elevated node could indeed connect me to the broader AustinMesh network. Height really is might.

Takeaway: a single elevated relay turned a hemmed-in valley node into one that reached multiple neighboring towns. Elevation beats power.

Field Notes

Lessons Learned

Wind can be a drag

Kite size, line length and weight, and wind speed all matter. My 5 ft delta and ~600 ft of line were right for my conditions; a larger or different-style kite might get better lift, making 800–1000 ft of line more optimal. Keep in mind that 1000 ft of line out does not mean 1000 ft above ground level. As I let line out and tracked the markers, I discovered I hadn't considered wind drag — at 1000 ft of line there was a significant drag curve keeping the kite from its highest potential. (Veritasium made a great video on drag.) After experimenting, I found a sweet spot around 600 ft of line, which had a lower drag curve and let the kite fly higher — confirmed by the kite node's GPS.

Reel size really matters

The line lets itself out easily as the wind pulls the kite up. It's reeling it back in that takes time and effort. The 8 in reel was just too small to be comfortable for average-size hands — I'd suggest the 10 in version or a higher-quality reel. I've also seen rigs that use a power drill to wind it up. It took me ~20 minutes to reel in 600 ft, with rests, so if you're in a time crunch, allow for the time it takes to bring the kite back down.

Attachment styles

In early test flights I had a 3D-printed enclosure tied with a string to the kite's line-anchor point. This was problematic: the node weighed ~60 g, wasn't watertight, and wasn't aerodynamic. It flopped around and spun itself around the kite line until the kite went into a death spiral. Could I send messages? Sure, but it wasn't great.

I moved the node into a plastic bag, reducing weight to 27 g. In its slim form I taped it to the line-anchor part of the kite. Taping all sides streamlined it, but the violent vibrations eventually broke the tape adhesion — about 1.5 hours before the tape ripped off and the node plunged to the ground. Later I taped it to the spreader pole on the topside, which has worked much better ever since.

Plan Ahead

Contingency

As mentioned, my relay node eventually came off the kite and it was some time before I noticed. Thankfully it had a GPS unit with precise location settings, so I was able to find it after it fell. It had landed in a detention pond, then floated to the shoreline.

What I learned here: make sure whatever the node is in is watertight (no holes in the bag), secure it to the kite well and consider a backup tether like string, and add an onboard GPS with precise location settings so you can recover a runaway node.
